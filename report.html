Experiment Design

Business Context & Metric Definitions

A B2B project-management SaaS product observed that users who do not activate in week 1 almost never return. The onboarding team hypothesised that a structured wizard would reduce friction and increase the proportion reaching meaningful product engagement.

⚠ Guardrail breached, highly significant Day-1 drop-off increased by +5.34 pp (p = 0.0003). The wizard's upfront multi-step commitment causes 1 in 19 additional users to abandon the product before returning. This is likely concentrated among low-intent users (e.g., speculative paid-ad clicks) who disengage when confronted with a mandatory flow before seeing the product's value.

Reconciling activation gain vs. drop-off increase

These findings are not contradictory, they describe different user populations:

Users who stay past day 1 Activate at a significantly higher rate in the wizard condition. The structured flow converts casual explorers into engaged users.
Additional users lost on day 1 A larger fraction of enrolled users never return, eroding the activation denominator. The wizard's friction acts as an early filter.

The net activation effect across all enrolled users (+22%) outweighs the drop-off cost, but day-1 churn is a leading indicator of long-term retention. The 30- and 90-day cohort retention of wizard-activated users must be monitored post-launch to confirm the trade-off holds over longer time horizons.

Section 9

CUPED, Variance Reduction

CUPED (Deng et al., 2013, Microsoft) reduces metric variance by removing the portion explained by pre-experiment covariates. Lower variance → tighter CIs → same true effect is easier to detect, equivalent to running a larger experiment for free.

Multi-covariate formula: Y_cuped = Y − X_c @ θ where θ = OLS(Y ~ X_c) and X_c is the column-centred covariate matrix. Variance reduction = R² of the OLS regression.

Why is the reduction modest (4.3%)? Revenue is 94%+ zeros. When nearly all variance comes from the binary zero/non-zero split, continuous covariates can only explain the small fraction of variance in the positive-revenue tail. CUPED is most powerful when: (a) the metric is continuous and low-noise, or (b) the covariate is a direct historical lag of the metric itself (e.g., "revenue last week"). On lower-variance metrics like session count or feature usage breadth, the same covariates would likely yield 15–30% reduction.

CUPED does not change the point estimate, only variance and CI width. This is guaranteed by the centring step: the adjusted metric has the same mean as the original.
